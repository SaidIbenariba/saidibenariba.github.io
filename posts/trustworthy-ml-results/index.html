Three habits that make ML results trustworthy

research-methodology
machine-learning
Seeds, ablations and error analysis: what they are, why reviewers check them, and how to do each in an afternoon.
Author

Said Ibenariba

Published

October 19, 2026

NoteTL;DR

A better number is not a result. Run several seeds to show it isn’t luck, ablate to show which part causes it, and do error analysis to show where it still fails.

The problem

Your new model beats the baseline by one point. You ran it once. Is that a result?

Not yet. One run can’t tell you whether the gain is real, which part of your method produced it, or what the model still gets wrong. Reviewers ask those three questions almost every time, and three habits answer them. None of them needs a cluster or a new idea. Each fits in an afternoon.

All examples below use a Darija speech recognition (ASR) setting, and all numbers are illustrative.

Habit 1: Seeds

Training is full of randomness: initial weights, data shuffling, dropout, sometimes the train/test split. A seed fixes the starting point of that randomness. Same seed, same “random” choices, so the run is reproducible.

import random, numpy as np, torch
SEED = 42
random.seed(SEED); np.random.seed(SEED); torch.manual_seed(SEED)
torch.cuda.manual_seed_all(SEED)  # if using GPU

Reproducible doesn’t mean representative, though. A single seed can be lucky. So run 3 to 5 seeds and report mean ± standard deviation:

Model Seed 1 Seed 2 Seed 3 Report
Baseline 80.1 79.5 80.4 80.0 ± 0.5
My method 81.9 79.8 80.6 80.8 ± 1.1

Compare best runs and you get 81.9 vs 80.4, a clear win. Compare across seeds and the gap (0.8) is smaller than the run-to-run spread (±1.1). The result is not convincing.

Do this:

  • Seed every random number generator (Python, NumPy, PyTorch, CUDA).
  • Use the same seeds for the baseline and your method, and report them.
  • Never report only the best seed.
  • For stronger claims, add a confidence interval or a significance test.

Habit 2: Ablations

The word comes from medicine: remove tissue to see what that part did. In ML, you remove one component of your method at a time and measure the drop. Without ablations you can say the whole method works, but not that each idea contributes.

Say the Darija ASR method has three additions: (A) code-switching data augmentation, (B) a shared French–Arabic tokenizer, (C) a language-ID auxiliary loss.

Configuration WER ↓ Takeaway
Full method (A+B+C) 18.2 —
− A (no augmentation) 22.5 A matters a lot (+4.3)
− B (no shared tokenizer) 18.9 B helps a little
− C (no language-ID loss) 18.3 C does ~nothing: drop it, or don’t claim it
Baseline (none) 23.0 —

The table turns “my model got a better number” into “augmentation is what improves results”. It also tells you to stop claiming C.

Do this:

  • Change one thing at a time, and keep everything else equal: data, tuning budget, seeds.
  • Map each ablation to one claim you want to make. Plan them before running.
  • Run ablations with multiple seeds. The 0.1 gap for C may be pure noise (see Habit 1).

Habit 3: Error analysis

A metric is one number. It tells you how much the model fails, not where or why. Error analysis means reading the mistakes by hand.

  1. Take 100–200 wrong predictions from the validation set (never test).
  2. Read each one: input, prediction, true label.
  3. Tag each with a cause. Invent the categories as you read.
  4. Count, and fix the largest category that is actually fixable.

Here is what 150 hand-read ASR errors might look like:

Error category Count % What it suggests
Code-switched French word 54 36% Code-switching augmentation
Background noise 33 22% Noise augmentation / denoising
Named entities 24 16% Contextual biasing / lexicon
Spelling variant of the same word 21 14% Not a real error: normalize before scoring
Wrong reference label 9 6% Fix the dataset
Other 9 6% —

Two lessons. The top category is your next experiment, and often a research hypothesis. And 20% of the “errors” are a measurement problem (spelling variants, bad labels), not a model problem.

Then go from qualitative to quantitative with slice analysis: compute the metric separately per subgroup.

Slice % of data WER ↓
Pure Darija 60% 14%
Darija + French code-switching 30% 27%
Noisy audio 10% 38%
Overall 100% ~20%

A 20% average looks fine. Code-switched speech is twice as bad, noisy audio nearly three times. That’s where the real problem is.

df.groupby("slice").apply(lambda g: metric(g.y_true, g.y_pred))

Do this:

  • Do it after every experiment, not just at the end.
  • Count, don’t just look. “Many errors are noisy audio” is weak; “22% are noisy audio” is evidence.
  • Report slices for both baseline and method, and check that no slice got worse.

What to take away

  • Seeds show the gain isn’t luck. Report mean ± std over 3–5 seeds, same seeds for every model.
  • Ablations show which part causes the gain. One component at a time, one claim per ablation.
  • Error analysis and slices show where the model still fails. That’s usually where the next idea comes from.

Where this doesn’t hold

  • Very large models where one run costs a fortune. Multiple seeds may be out of reach, so say so and widen your error bars in words.
  • Tiny slices give noisy numbers. Report slice size and a confidence interval, or merge slices.
  • Hand-reading errors doesn’t scale to every experiment. Sample, and keep the categories consistent across runs.
